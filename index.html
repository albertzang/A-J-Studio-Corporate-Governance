4. Share Structure

To separate decision-making rights from dividend rights, the company uses two classes of shares. This is a draft design to be finalized in the Articles with a lawyer.

Class Rights Albert Jacob Purpose
Class A — Voting Voting; non-participating (no/nominal dividend) 60 votes 40 votes Control (60/40)
Class B — Dividend Non-voting; entitled to dividends and return of capital 50% 50% Economics (50/50)
BCA — Supported

The BCA does not use fixed "common/preferred" categories; instead, each share carries the special rights or restrictions set in the Articles, which may relate to voting, dividends, and return of capital (BCA ss. 59–60 and the definition of "special rights or restrictions"). Multiple classes with different voting and dividend rights are therefore permitted.

Constraint: within a single class, every share must carry the same rights (s. 59(4)). That is why the 60/40 control and 50/50 economics are achieved with two classes rather than one. Once shares are issued, changing a class's rights needs a special separate resolution of that class (s. 61).

Draft — confirm class terms, amounts, and paid-up capital with a lawyer/CPA.

5. Decision-Making & Voting

Two decision thresholds apply at the shareholder level:

  • Non-major mattersOrdinary resolution — simple majority (> 50% of votes cast). Albert's 60% can carry these alone.
  • Major mattersSuper-majority — at least 75% of votes cast (see the draft list in §6).
  • DividendsPaid on Class B, split 50/50 (see §8).
BCA — Supported (with articles set to 3/4)

Under the BCA, the "special majority" needed for a special resolution may be set in the Articles at no less than 2/3 and no more than 3/4 of votes cast (default 2/3). A 75% (3/4) threshold is therefore the maximum allowed and can be entrenched in the Articles for major matters. Ordinary matters pass by simple majority (> 50%).

Attention — practical effect & board vs shareholders

With a 60/40 split, neither founder reaches 75% alone, so major matters effectively require both founders' consent (functionally unanimous). Ordinary matters (> 50%) can be carried by Albert alone.

These weighted thresholds work at the shareholder level (votes attached to shares). At the two-person board, the BCA gives each director one vote, so the board can deadlock and cannot simply be "weighted" 60/40. Reserve major/non-major decisions to shareholder resolutions, tailor the Articles, and put a deadlock-breaking mechanism in a shareholders' agreement. Note: unlike the federal CBCA, the BC BCA has no statutory "unanimous shareholders' agreement" that strips directors of their duties — use a contractual shareholders' agreement plus tailored Articles.

BCA — Supported

A BC company needs at least one director (s. 120), and BC imposes no residency or citizenship requirement on directors (s. 124) — both founders can serve. Directors owe fiduciary and care duties to the company.

8. Meetings, Profit & Dividends

Each year the company holds an Annual General Meeting (AGM), at which the shareholders decide how to use the year's net profit — the amount left after operating, infrastructure, labour, and all other costs.

Annual allocation of net profit

  • Company development reserve — set aside an amount each year to buffer future uncertainty and growth
  • Dividends — distribute the remainder per the dividend ratio (Class B, 50/50)
Attention — solvency test on dividends

Under BCA s. 70, the company must not declare or pay a dividend if there are reasonable grounds to believe it is insolvent (unable to pay debts as they come due) or that the dividend would render it insolvent; it also needs sufficient retained earnings. Directors who approve an offside dividend can be personally liable. Dividends must follow class rights — equal within Class B, i.e., 50/50.

The "development reserve" is an internal retained-earnings allocation (a bookkeeping decision), not a separate legal entity.

Ways to structure the injection (draft options)

MethodHow it is recordedNotes
Share subscription (equity) Increases paid-up capital (PUC) Return of capital up to PUC is generally tax-free but reduces PUC; equity is harder to withdraw (needs return of capital, redemption, or dividend).
Shareholder loan (debt) Recorded as a company liability Repaid later as return of principal (not taxable); can be interest-free. A loan from a shareholder to the company is not caught by the ITA s. 15(2) shareholder-loan rules (those target loans the other way). Most flexible for small amounts.
Hybrid (recommended draft) Nominal share subscription + shareholder loan for the balance Sets the share structure while keeping PUC low and allowing flexible, tax-free repayment of the loan portion.
Legal / accounting / tax

Document everything: subscription agreements, a directors' resolution issuing the shares, and shareholder-loan agreements, all recorded in the minute book and the books of account. A capital injection is not revenue, so no GST/PST applies. Keep the paid-up capital deliberate (it affects future tax-free capital returns), and track each founder's contributions and loans in a register. Confirm the equity-vs-loan mix and PUC with a CPA.

Also confirm current Wealthsimple business-account eligibility/terms; keep an alternative business bank in mind as a fallback.

10. Revenue Sharing & Founder Compensation

All contract revenue is first paid into the company's Wealthsimple Business account. For each project, the founders agree a reasonable split reflecting each person's contribution across lead generation/negotiation, development, delivery, and after-sales support, and each draws an agreed amount to their personal account. The remaining funds stay in the company: part covers other project costs, and the rest becomes company profit for the AGM (§8).

Attention — this is the key compliance/tax area

Contribution-based, variable payments to the founders cannot be treated as dividends: dividends within a share class must be equal per share (s. 59(4)), and the Class B dividend is fixed at 50/50 — so effort-based, unequal amounts must be characterized as compensation for services, not dividends.

Proper options: (a) Salary/wages — needs a CRA payroll account and source deductions (income tax, CPP; EI generally does not apply to owners with > 40% voting control) with T4 slips; or (b) Contractor / management fees — each founder invoices the company and reports self-employment income (consider GST registration). Both are deductible business expenses to the company.

Avoid undocumented "withdrawals" to personal accounts: every draw must be classified (salary, contractor fee, expense reimbursement, dividend, or shareholder-loan repayment) and recorded, or it risks being taxed as a shareholder benefit / deemed dividend (ITA s. 15). Recommendation: adopt a written compensation policy, pay effort-based splits as salary or contractor fees, keep dividends separate and equal (50/50), reconcile monthly, and have a CPA set up payroll/contractor processes.
