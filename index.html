A&J Studio — Corporate Governance

Not legal, tax, or accounting advice. This document is a private working record. The compliance notes summarize the BC Business Corporations Act (BCA) and general practice as of September 2026, but you should confirm the structure with a BC corporate lawyer and a CPA before incorporating and before implementing the compensation and dividend arrangements.

3.2 Share structure

The company uses two share classes, with a maximum of one share in each. Albert Z. holds the Class A share. Jacob W. holds the Class B share. There is no unissued share. The class rights and the major-matter rule are written into the Articles (see §12).

Class Rights Holder
Class A Carries the right to vote at general meetings. Dividends on this class. The company must not do a major matter without this class's written consent. Albert Z., the one authorized share
Class B Does not carry the right to vote at general meetings. Dividends on this class. The company must not do a major matter without this class's written consent. Jacob W., the one authorized share

A dividend resolution may set a different amount on each class, including an amount on one class and none on the other. Every share in a class carries the same rights (BCA s. 59(4)), which is why each founder has his own class. A future partner needs a change to this structure first, such as raising a maximum or creating a class, and then an issue or a transfer. Each of those steps is a major matter (§5).

4. Decision-Making & Voting

Three different records cover three different kinds of decision. The same person may sign more than one of them. The minute book should name which one it is (see §13).

  • Ordinary mattersAlbert Z. alone, as sole director. If the Act requires a shareholder resolution, he also passes it alone because his Class A share carries the right to vote at general meetings, and Class B does not. Jacob W.'s agreement is not required.
  • Major mattersWritten consent of both classes, Class A and Class B (see §5). Exercising Class A's right to vote at general meetings does not replace Jacob W.'s Class B consent, and Jacob W. cannot replace Albert Z.'s Class A consent.
  • DividendsDeclaring a dividend is a major matter. The resolution sets the amount on Class A and the amount on Class B. Those amounts may differ (see §6 and §8).

4.1 Board meetings

A board meeting is a meeting of the directors. The directors manage the company, or supervise that management (BCA s. 136). They appoint officers and set their duties (s. 141). Ordinary business is decided here: client work, delivery, spending inside the ordinary course, and the other items that are not on the major-matter list.

This company has one director, Albert Z. Under s. 140(4), the sole director may constitute a board meeting by himself. He may also sign a written directors' resolution instead of holding a meeting (s. 140(3)). There is no second director and no tie to break.

Jacob W. does not attend as a director and has no vote at a board meeting. His President and CTO work is officer work done under the board's authority (§3). It is not a board vote.

4.2 Shareholder meetings

A shareholder meeting is a meeting of the holders of shares that carry the right to vote on that matter. Shareholders do not manage the day-to-day business. They decide items the Act or the Articles reserve to them, and they elect the director. The company holds an annual general meeting unless the shareholders entitled to vote at it waive it (s. 182, and §6).

At a general meeting, only Class A carries the right to vote. Albert Z. holds that share, so he alone can pass the resolution. Class B does not carry that right. Jacob W.'s absence cannot stop the meeting, and his disagreement is not a vote on that resolution.

Albert Z. is often the only person involved in both a board decision and a shareholder decision. They are still different acts. A directors' resolution is him acting as director. A Class A shareholder resolution is him exercising Class A's right to vote at general meetings. Each one is written down separately.

4.3 Veto consents

A major matter is not a board vote and not an exercise of Class A's right to vote at general meetings. The Articles provide that the company must not do it unless both classes consent in writing. Class A is Albert Z.'s consent. Class B is Jacob W.'s consent. Each class has one holder, so one signature is that class's consent, and the other person's signature cannot replace it. Albert Z. may sign a Class A shareholder resolution and, on another paper, the Class A veto consent. They are different records (see §13).

The two consents are two classes because every share inside one class must have the same rights (s. 59(4)). If both founders held one class, one founder might pass a class resolution without the other. Separate classes mean each consent stands on its own. The dividend sits on the same class as that founder's consent.

Attention — have the lawyer entrench both vetoes

The Articles need to say that the sole director cannot do a listed major matter without both consents. Section 137 allows the Articles to move particular director powers. Section 61 protects a class when its own rights are changed. The drafting has to close the path on which Albert Z. uses Class A's right to vote at general meetings, or holds a meeting Jacob W. skips, and thereby drops the Class B veto. Both classes are issued on the day of incorporation so that neither consent is something the other founder can withhold later.

A shareholders' agreement can repeat the list and set the remedy if someone ignores it. British Columbia has no federal-style unanimous shareholders' agreement that removes directors' duties. Albert Z., as director, still has to act honestly and in the company's interests, with reasonable care (s. 142).

Informational only — not legal advice. A BC corporate lawyer should draft the Articles before incorporation.

6. Meetings, Profit & Dividends

Each year the company holds an Annual General Meeting unless every shareholder whose share carries the right to vote at general meetings waives it (BCA s. 182). Albert Z. holds all of those shares, so he can hold the meeting alone or sign the waiver. The meeting is for the business the Act puts to voting shareholders, such as the annual financial statements and election of the director. It is not a board meeting, and Jacob W. does not vote at it.

6.1 Annual allocation of net profit

  • Company development reserve — setting the reserve is a major matter, so both classes must consent (§5)
  • Dividends — declaring a dividend is a major matter. The resolution sets the dollars on Class A and the dollars on Class B, and those amounts may differ (see §8)
Attention — solvency test on dividends

Under BCA s. 70, the company must not declare or pay a dividend if there are reasonable grounds to believe it is insolvent (unable to pay debts as they come due) or that the dividend would render it insolvent; it also needs sufficient retained earnings. Directors who approve an offside dividend can be personally liable. Inside one class every share is paid the same amount, which follows because each founder owns every share of his own class. Declaring the dividend is a major matter, so both classes consent before it is paid (§5). If the solvency test fails, neither founder is paid for that period.

The "development reserve" is an internal retained-earnings allocation (a bookkeeping decision), not a separate legal entity.

8. Founder Pay

All contract revenue is first paid into the company's Wealthsimple Business account (see §2.2). The company pays its own costs from that account. Money paid to a founder for his work is a dividend on his own dividend class, and nothing else.

8.1 Dividends only

The company must not pay either founder a salary, wage, bonus, director's fee, or management fee, and a founder does not invoice the company. Expense reimbursements, and repayment of the shareholder loans in §7, stay allowed. Those are not pay for work.

Albert Z. owns every Class A share. Jacob W. owns every Class B share. One dividend resolution sets the amount on each class. The amounts may differ, and the resolution may pay one class and none on the other. Every share inside a class is paid the same amount (s. 59(4)), which is already true because each founder owns his whole class.

Declaring the dividend is a major matter, so both classes consent before it is paid (§5). Albert Z., as director, still applies the solvency test (§6). If that test fails, the company does not pay either founder for that period.

The ban is written in the Articles. Amending it, or paying a founder anything other than a dividend on his class, is a major matter, so neither founder can turn salary on alone. The shareholders' agreement repeats the ban, so each founder can enforce it against the other. The Articles do not adopt the Table 1 clause that lets directors set their own remuneration.

The ban binds the two founders. If the facts of the work are employment, the Canada Revenue Agency or the Employment Standards Branch can still require payroll. Dividends do not build CPP or EI, and the company cannot deduct them. Have a CPA confirm this pattern before the first dividend. Record every payment as a dividend, an expense reimbursement, or a loan repayment. An unclassified draw can be taxed as a shareholder benefit (Income Tax Act s. 15).

Informational only — not tax or legal advice. Have a lawyer write the class rights and the ban into the Articles.

12.1 What is filed, and what is kept

British Columbia does not use a separate company bylaw. The internal rules are the Articles. The online incorporation filing does not ask you to upload them. Before the filing continues, you certify that two documents already exist: a signed Incorporation Agreement and a set of Articles. Both stay in the minute book. Filing without them can leave the incorporation invalid.

What you submit through Corporate Online is the Incorporation Application and the Notice of Articles. The Notice of Articles is the short public record: name, registered office, directors, and the authorized share structure. After filing, the registry sends the Certificate of Incorporation, a certified Incorporation Application, and a certified Notice of Articles.

The ready-made rulebook is Table 1 in the Business Corporations Regulation (B.C. Reg. 65/2004, Part 15). Its purpose is to supply the ordinary operating rules — meetings, directors, officers, records, and a simple share regime — so a small company does not have to draft those clauses itself. The province's incorporation steps treat Table 1 as suitable when it is adopted unchanged. It does not create special share classes or a personal veto.

12.2 Articles for this company

This company needs one set of Articles, drafted with a BC corporate lawyer. Unmodified Table 1 is the wrong set to adopt. The sample Incorporation Agreement above only adopts Table 1 as written, so it is a pattern, not the document to sign.

The Articles have to state, in one document:

  • The two classes and their rights: Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings, receives Albert Z.'s dividend, and carries his veto; Class B does not carry the right to vote at general meetings, receives Jacob W.'s dividend, and carries his veto (see §3.2).
  • The Notice of Articles authorizes a maximum of one Class A share and one Class B share, without par value. The Incorporation Agreement issues the Class A share to Albert Z. and the Class B share to Jacob W.
  • A dividend may be declared on Class A, on Class B, or on both, in different amounts (see §8).
  • The company must not pay either founder a salary, wage, bonus, director's fee, or management fee, and must not adopt the Table 1 clause that lets directors set their own remuneration. Changing that ban is a major matter.
  • Only Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings. Albert Z. holds that share (see §4).
  • Major matters. The Articles name the list in §5 and state that the company must not do any item on it unless both Class A and Class B have consented in writing. A directors' resolution does not satisfy that requirement. Class A's right to vote at general meetings does not replace the Class B consent, and Jacob W. cannot replace the Class A consent (see §4).
  • Ordinary mechanics may follow Table 1 where they do not conflict: how a directors' resolution is passed, how officers are appointed, and how records are kept.

The Incorporation Agreement is a separate signed paper. Each incorporator agrees to take the shares set out in it, and the agreement adopts this custom set of Articles. It is kept with the Articles. It is not uploaded with the filing.

Informational only — not legal advice. Have a BC corporate lawyer draft the Articles and the Incorporation Agreement before the filing certification.
