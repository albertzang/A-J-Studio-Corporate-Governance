A&J Studio — Corporate Governance

An internal reference describing how A&J Studio — a corporation to be incorporated under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia, Canada) — is owned, financed, and operated by its two co-founders.

Status: Working draft · Jurisdiction: British Columbia, Canada · Co-founders: Albert Z. & Jacob W.

Not legal, tax, or accounting advice. This document is a private working record. The compliance notes summarize the BC Business Corporations Act (BCA) and general practice as of September 2026, but you should confirm the structure with a BC corporate lawyer and a CPA before incorporating and before implementing the dividend and shareholder-loan arrangements.
Principles
  1. The company is intended to remain a two-founder company. The share structure is not expected to admit a later partner or shareholder. Each founder holds a separate class, and each class has one share only. Those shares are not to be diluted (see §2.2).
  2. Albert Z. is the sole director and the only holder of shares that carry the right to vote at general meetings. He decides ordinary matters alone, by a written resolution issued when one is needed. Day-to-day decisions therefore do not deadlock, and the company does not hold board or shareholder meetings (see §3).
  3. Both founders have an equal veto on major corporate matters. Neither can override the other (see §4).
  4. The only way a founder may support the company financially is an interest-free shareholder loan. No other form is allowed (see §5).
  5. The only way the two founders share net profit is by dividend. No other form is allowed (see §6).

2. Founders & Share Structure

The company has two co-founders. Each person's titles and duties are in §2.1. The two shares are in §2.2.

2.1 Roles and responsibilities

Albert Z. is the sole director. Jacob W. is not a director. The officer titles are management roles. They do not add a board vote, and they do not replace either class consent.

Person Title Responsibility
Albert Z. Director Sole director. Decides ordinary matters by written resolution. Major matters need both class consents (see §4).
Albert Z. CEO Strategy and vision; external relationships; accountability for ordinary management.
Albert Z. Secretary Minute book and registers; notices, resolutions, and minutes; filings.
Jacob W. CTO Technical leadership; architecture; engineering standards; security.
Jacob W. President Day-to-day operations, delivery, and internal management.

Signing authority is still to be decided.

2.2 Share structure

The company uses two share classes, with a maximum of one share in each. Albert Z. holds the Class A share. Jacob W. holds the Class B share. There is no unissued share. The class rights and the major-matter rule are written into the Articles (see §9).

Class Rights Holder
Class A

  • Dividends on this class.
  • Veto on major matters.

Class B
  • Does not carry the right to vote at general meetings.
  • Dividends on this class.
  • Veto on major matters.

Every share in a class carries the same rights (BCA s. 59(4)), so each founder has his own class. The design does not admit another shareholder, and neither class is diluted. Changing that structure is a major matter (§4).

Draft — confirm class terms with a lawyer before filing.

3. Ordinary Decisions

Albert Z. decides ordinary matters alone, in writing. The company does not hold a board meeting or a shareholder meeting. The two records below are different papers, and the minute book names which one it is (see §11). A major matter is not decided here (see §4).

3.1 Directors' resolutions

The directors manage the company, or supervise that management (BCA s. 136). This company does not hold board meetings. Albert Z., the sole director, decides ordinary matters by a written directors' resolution (s. 140(3)): client work, delivery, and spending in the ordinary course. There is no second director, so those decisions do not deadlock.

Jacob W. does not sign a directors' resolution. President and CTO work is officer work under the director's authority (§2.1).

3.2 Class A shareholder resolutions

The company does not hold shareholder meetings, including the annual general meeting. Only Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings, and Albert Z. holds that share. When the Act requires a shareholder act, such as the annual financial statements or election of the director, he signs a written Class A resolution instead of holding the meeting. Each year that resolution also waives the annual general meeting (s. 182).

Class B does not carry the right to vote, so Jacob W. does not sign these resolutions. A directors' resolution and a Class A shareholder resolution are different records, even when Albert Z. signs both (see §11).

4. Major Matters

The Articles adopt this list (see §9). Each item needs the written consent of both classes: Albert Z.'s Class A and Jacob W.'s Class B. Neither founder can give the other's consent, and a Class A shareholder resolution does not replace it. Everything not on this list is an ordinary matter, which Albert Z. decides alone (§3).

  • Amending the Notice of Articles or Articles, including class rights, the one-share maximum, the fiscal year end, the ban on founder salary, or the shareholder-loan rule
  • Issuing or transferring a share, admitting a shareholder, or a founder departure or buy-out
  • Approving or amending the shareholders' agreement
  • Declaring a dividend, including the amount on each class, or setting the company development reserve
  • Signing a client contract
  • Selling or assigning the company's intellectual property, other than a license to a client in the ordinary course
  • Selling the company, amalgamating or merging it, or winding it up
  • Changing the company's bank, or who may sign on an account
  • Appointing or removing an officer
  • Starting or ending a line of business
  • Commencing or settling litigation

A lawyer writes this list into the Articles before incorporation.

4.1 Veto consents

A major matter needs the written consent of both classes. It is not a directors' resolution and not a Class A shareholder resolution. Class A is Albert Z.'s consent. Class B is Jacob W.'s consent. One signature is that class's consent, and the other founder cannot give it. Albert Z. may sign both a Class A shareholder resolution and, on a separate paper, the Class A veto consent (see §11).

The consents are separate classes because every share in one class must carry the same rights (s. 59(4)). One shared class could let one founder consent without the other. Each founder's dividend sits on the same class as his consent.

Attention — have the lawyer entrench both vetoes

The Articles need to say that the sole director cannot do a listed major matter without both consents. Section 137 allows the Articles to move particular director powers. Section 61 protects a class when its own rights are changed. The drafting has to close the path on which Albert Z. uses a Class A resolution to drop the Class B veto. Both classes are issued on the day of incorporation so that neither consent is something the other founder can withhold later.

A shareholders' agreement can repeat the list and set the remedy if someone ignores it. British Columbia has no federal-style unanimous shareholders' agreement that removes directors' duties. Albert Z., as director, still has to act honestly and in the company's interests, with reasonable care (s. 142).

As part of this consultation, please have the reviewing lawyer prepare the standard template or templates this calls for.

Informational only — not legal advice. A BC corporate lawyer should draft the Articles before incorporation.

5. Shareholder Loans

The only way a founder may support the company financially is an interest-free shareholder loan. No other form is allowed. He must not pay money in for a further share, as a capital contribution, as a gift, or on any other basis. The one share each founder already holds is not that support.

The founder who lends decides the amount, and he lends only if he chooses to. Neither founder has to match the other. The amount does not set control. Albert Z.'s ordinary vote comes from holding the Class A share. The company records each loan as a liability. Repayment returns principal and is not taxable.

Each founder has chosen $1,500 as his first advance. Each signed copy of the agreement below has one lender, and that lender is either Albert Z. or Jacob W.

While the company is carrying on, a loan is repaid when that lender demands it or when the company chooses to repay, if the company has the money. On a winding-up, other creditors are paid first. If what remains is not enough to repay both founders' loans in full, the two loans rank equally and the money is split in proportion to the unpaid principal. If $1,000 remains, Albert Z. is owed $3,000, and Jacob W. is owed $1,000, Albert Z. receives $750 and Jacob W. receives $250. If the company can repay both in full, each receives his unpaid principal.

This rule is written into the Articles (see §9). Changing it is a major matter (§4).

SHAREHOLDER LOAN AGREEMENT This Agreement is made on [DATE]. Lender: [Yansong Zang (Albert Z.) or Yiji Wang (Jacob W.)] [address] Borrower: [Company legal name], a British Columbia company [registered office address] The Lender is one person, and is either Yansong Zang (Albert Z.) or Yiji Wang (Jacob W.). 1. LOAN The Lender chooses whether to lend, and chooses the amount. The first advance is CAD $1,500. Any further advance is whatever amount the Lender chooses to pay to the Borrower's business bank account. The Lender is not required to match any other founder. 2. INTEREST Every advance is interest-free. No interest accrues at any time. 3. REPAYMENT The Borrower will repay the outstanding principal on written demand by the Lender, or earlier if the Borrower chooses. There is no penalty for early repayment. Repayment is a return of principal only. 4. WINDING-UP On a winding-up, other creditors are paid first. If what remains cannot repay every founder's shareholder loan in full, those loans rank equally. Each founder receives the fraction of the remaining money equal to his unpaid principal divided by the total unpaid principal of both founders. 5. NO SECURITY The Loan is unsecured. 6. RECORDS The Borrower will record the Loan as a liability and keep a running balance in its books. 7. GOVERNING LAW This Agreement is governed by the laws of British Columbia. Signed: Lender: ____________________ Date: __________ (Yansong Zang or Yiji Wang) Borrower: ____________________ Date: __________

6. Dividends

All contract revenue is paid into the company's Wealthsimple Business account (see §7.2). The company pays its own costs from that account. The only way the two founders share net profit is by dividend on each founder's own class. No other form is allowed.

The company must not pay either founder a salary, wage, bonus, director's fee, or management fee, and a founder does not invoice the company. An expense reimbursement and a loan repayment (§5) are not a share of profit.

Declaring the dividend is a major matter, so both classes consent before it is paid (§4). The amount on each class may differ. Albert Z., as director, still applies the solvency test.

Attention — solvency test on dividends

Under BCA s. 70, the company must not declare or pay a dividend if there are reasonable grounds to believe it is insolvent, or that the dividend would render it insolvent. A director who approves an offside dividend can be personally liable. If the test fails, the company declares no dividend for that period.

Setting the company development reserve is also a major matter. The reserve is an internal allocation of retained earnings, not a separate legal entity.

The ban is in the Articles. Changing it means amending the Articles, which is a major matter (§4), so neither founder can lift it alone. The shareholders' agreement repeats the ban. The Articles do not adopt the Table 1 clause that lets directors set their own remuneration.

The ban binds the two founders. If the facts of the work are employment, the Canada Revenue Agency or the Employment Standards Branch can still require payroll. Dividends do not build CPP or EI, and the company cannot deduct them. Have a CPA confirm this pattern before the first dividend. Record every payment as a dividend, an expense reimbursement, or a loan repayment. An unclassified draw can be taxed as a shareholder benefit (Income Tax Act s. 15).

Informational only — not tax or legal advice. Have a lawyer write the class rights and the ban into the Articles.

9. Articles

The Articles lock in the principles above. The filing does not upload them.

9.1 Incorporation filing

British Columbia does not use a separate company bylaw. The internal rules are the Articles. The online incorporation filing does not ask you to upload them. Before the filing continues, you certify that two documents already exist: a signed Incorporation Agreement and a set of Articles. Both stay in the minute book. Filing without them can leave the incorporation invalid.

What you submit through Corporate Online is the Incorporation Application and the Notice of Articles. The Notice of Articles is the short public record: name, registered office, directors, and the authorized share structure. After filing, the registry sends the Certificate of Incorporation, a certified Incorporation Application, and a certified Notice of Articles.

Table 1 of the Business Corporations Regulation (B.C. Reg. 65/2004, Part 15) is the default rulebook. The province's incorporation steps treat it as suitable when adopted unchanged. It does not create these two classes, the vetoes, or the decision to act by written resolution instead of a meeting.

9.2 What the Articles state

This company needs one set of Articles, drafted with a BC corporate lawyer. Unmodified Table 1 is the wrong set to adopt. The sample Incorporation Agreement above only adopts Table 1 as written, so it is a pattern, not the document to sign.

The Articles have to state, in one document:

  • The two classes and their rights: Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings, receives Albert Z.'s dividend, and carries his veto; Class B does not carry the right to vote at general meetings, receives Jacob W.'s dividend, and carries his veto (see §2.2).
  • The Notice of Articles authorizes a maximum of one Class A share and one Class B share, without par value. The Incorporation Agreement issues the Class A share to Albert Z. and the Class B share to Jacob W.
  • A dividend may be declared on Class A, on Class B, or on both, in different amounts (see §6).
  • The company must not pay either founder a salary, wage, bonus, director's fee, or management fee, and must not adopt the Table 1 clause that lets directors set their own remuneration. Changing that ban is a major matter.
  • The only way a founder may support the company financially is an interest-free shareholder loan. No other form is allowed. The founder chooses whether to lend and the amount. On a winding-up, if both loans cannot be repaid in full, they rank equally and the remainder is split in proportion to unpaid principal (see §5). Changing this rule is a major matter.
  • Only Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings. Albert Z. holds that share (see §3).
  • Major matters. The Articles name the list in §4 and state that the company must not do any item on it unless both Class A and Class B have consented in writing. A directors' resolution does not satisfy that requirement. Class A's right to vote at general meetings does not replace the Class B consent, and Jacob W. cannot replace the Class A consent (see §4.1).
  • Ordinary mechanics may follow Table 1 only where they do not conflict with these principles. Board and shareholder business is done by written resolution, not by a meeting.

The Incorporation Agreement is a separate signed paper. Each incorporator agrees to take the shares set out in it, and the agreement adopts this custom set of Articles. It is kept with the Articles. It is not uploaded with the filing.

Informational only — not legal advice. Have a BC corporate lawyer draft the Articles and the Incorporation Agreement before the filing certification.

11. Minute Book

The minute book records decisions. The company does not hold board meetings or shareholder meetings. It keeps the constating documents and three sets of forms: a directors' resolution (§3.1), a Class A shareholder resolution (§3.2), and a veto consent (§4.1). A page names its set. Albert Z. may sign more than one set for the same event: as director, as the Class A shareholder, and as the Class A veto. The samples are drafts for the lawyer to adapt.

11.1 Directors' resolution form

A directors' resolution records Albert Z.'s decision as sole director. He passes it in writing, in place of a meeting (BCA s. 140(3)). The scope is ordinary management. A listed major matter does not belong here.

Do not use this form for a matter on the major-matter list.] This resolution is an act of the director only. 11.3 Veto consent form

A veto consent is the written consent of one class to one listed major matter (§4). Class A is Albert Z.'s consent. Class B is Jacob W.'s consent. Each class is its own record. The company does not act on the matter until both consents are signed. Neither consent is a general-meeting vote, and Albert Z.'s right to vote at general meetings cannot stand in for Jacob W.'s Class B consent.

Date: [DATE] Class: [Class A — Yansong Zang (Albert Z.) / Class B — Yiji Wang (Jacob W.)] Matter: [Name the item from the major-matter list, and the specific decision.] The holder of the class named above consents to that matter. This paper is that class's veto consent only. It is not a directors' resolution and it is not a Class A shareholder resolution. Signed: ______________________________ [Name], holder of all the issued shares of the class named above
