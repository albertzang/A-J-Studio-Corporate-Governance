3. Founders & Share Structure

The company has two co-founders. Each person's titles and duties are in §3.1. The two shares are in §3.2.

Albert Z. is the sole director. Jacob W. is not a director. The officer titles are management roles. They do not add a board vote, and they do not replace either class consent.

3.2 Share structure

The company uses two share classes, with a maximum of one share in each. Albert Z. holds the Class A share. Jacob W. holds the Class B share. There is no unissued share. The class rights and the major-matter rule are written into the Articles (see §11).

Class Rights Holder
Class A Carries the right to vote at general meetings. Dividends on this class. The company must not do a major matter without this class's written consent. Albert Z., the one authorized share
Class B Does not carry the right to vote at general meetings. Dividends on this class. The company must not do a major matter without this class's written consent. Jacob W., the one authorized share

A dividend resolution may set a different amount on each class, including an amount on one class and none on the other. Every share in a class carries the same rights (BCA s. 59(4)), which is why each founder has his own class. A future partner needs a change to this structure first, such as raising a maximum or creating a class, and then an issue or a transfer. Each of those steps is a major matter (§5).

4. Decision-Making & Voting

Three different records cover three different kinds of decision. The same person may sign more than one of them. The minute book should name which one it is (see §12).

  • Ordinary mattersAlbert Z. alone, as sole director, by a written directors' resolution. If the Act requires a shareholder resolution, he signs that too, because his Class A share carries the right to vote at general meetings and Class B does not. The company does not hold a meeting for either one. Jacob W.'s agreement is not required.
  • Major mattersWritten consent of both classes, Class A and Class B (see §5). Exercising Class A's right to vote at general meetings does not replace Jacob W.'s Class B consent, and Jacob W. cannot replace Albert Z.'s Class A consent.
  • DividendsDeclaring a dividend is a major matter. The resolution sets the amount on Class A and the amount on Class B. Those amounts may differ (see §4.4 and §7).

4.1 Directors' resolutions

The directors manage the company, or supervise that management (BCA s. 136). This company does not hold board meetings. Albert Z. is the sole director, so he decides ordinary management by a written directors' resolution (s. 140(3)): client work, delivery, and spending in the ordinary course. There is no second director and no tie to break.

Jacob W. does not sign a directors' resolution. His President and CTO work is officer work done under the director's authority (§3).

4.2 Shareholder resolutions

The company does not hold shareholder meetings, including the annual general meeting. Only Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings, and Albert Z. holds that share. When the Act requires a shareholder act, such as the annual financial statements or election of the director, he signs a written Class A resolution instead of holding the meeting. Each year that resolution also waives the annual general meeting (s. 182).

Class B does not carry the right to vote, so Jacob W. does not sign these resolutions. A directors' resolution and a Class A shareholder resolution are different records, even when Albert Z. signs both (see §12).

4.3 Veto consents

A major matter is not a board vote and not an exercise of Class A's right to vote at general meetings. The Articles provide that the company must not do it unless both classes consent in writing. Class A is Albert Z.'s consent. Class B is Jacob W.'s consent. Each class has one holder, so one signature is that class's consent, and the other person's signature cannot replace it. Albert Z. may sign a Class A shareholder resolution and, on another paper, the Class A veto consent. They are different records (see §12).

The two consents are two classes because every share inside one class must have the same rights (s. 59(4)). If both founders held one class, one founder might pass a class resolution without the other. Separate classes mean each consent stands on its own. The dividend sits on the same class as that founder's consent.

Attention — have the lawyer entrench both vetoes

The Articles need to say that the sole director cannot do a listed major matter without both consents. Section 137 allows the Articles to move particular director powers. Section 61 protects a class when its own rights are changed. The drafting has to close the path on which Albert Z. uses a Class A resolution to drop the Class B veto. Both classes are issued on the day of incorporation so that neither consent is something the other founder can withhold later.

A shareholders' agreement can repeat the list and set the remedy if someone ignores it. British Columbia has no federal-style unanimous shareholders' agreement that removes directors' duties. Albert Z., as director, still has to act honestly and in the company's interests, with reasonable care (s. 142).

Informational only — not legal advice. A BC corporate lawyer should draft the Articles before incorporation.

4.4 Dividends

Declaring a dividend is a major matter. The resolution sets the dollars on Class A and the dollars on Class B, and those amounts may differ (see §7). Both classes consent before it is paid (§5).

Attention — solvency test on dividends

Under BCA s. 70, the company must not declare or pay a dividend if there are reasonable grounds to believe it is insolvent, or that the dividend would render it insolvent. A director who approves an offside dividend can be personally liable. If the test fails, neither founder is paid for that period.

Setting the company development reserve is also a major matter. The reserve is an internal allocation of retained earnings, not a separate legal entity.

6. Shareholder Loans

A shareholder loan is the only way a founder may put money into the company. He must not pay money in for a further share, as a capital contribution, as a gift, or on any other basis. The one share each founder already holds is not a capital injection.

Every such loan is interest-free. The founder who lends decides the amount, and he lends only if he chooses to. Neither founder has to match the other. The dollars do not set control. Albert Z.'s ordinary vote comes from holding the Class A share. The company records each loan as a liability. Repayment returns principal and is not taxable.

Each founder has chosen $1,500 as his first advance. Each signed copy of the agreement below has one lender, and that lender is either Albert Z. or Jacob W.

While the company is carrying on, a loan is repaid when that lender demands it or when the company chooses to repay, if the company has the money. On a winding-up, other creditors are paid first. If what remains is not enough to repay both founders' loans in full, the two loans rank equally and the money is split in proportion to the unpaid principal. If $1,000 remains, Albert Z. is owed $3,000, and Jacob W. is owed $1,000, Albert Z. receives $750 and Jacob W. receives $250. If the company can repay both in full, each receives his unpaid principal.

This rule is written into the Articles (see §11). Changing it is a major matter (§5).

SHAREHOLDER LOAN AGREEMENT This Agreement is made on [DATE]. Lender: [Yansong Zang (Albert Z.) or Yiji Wang (Jacob W.)] [address] Borrower: [Company legal name], a British Columbia company [registered office address] The Lender is one person, and is either Yansong Zang (Albert Z.) or Yiji Wang (Jacob W.). 1. LOAN The Lender chooses whether to lend, and chooses the amount. The first advance is CAD $1,500. Any further advance is whatever amount the Lender chooses to pay to the Borrower's business bank account. The Lender is not required to match any other founder. 2. INTEREST Every advance is interest-free. No interest accrues at any time. 3. REPAYMENT The Borrower will repay the outstanding principal on written demand by the Lender, or earlier if the Borrower chooses. There is no penalty for early repayment. Repayment is a return of principal only. 4. WINDING-UP On a winding-up, other creditors are paid first. If what remains cannot repay every founder's shareholder loan in full, those loans rank equally. Each founder receives the fraction of the remaining money equal to his unpaid principal divided by the total unpaid principal of both founders. 5. NO SECURITY The Loan is unsecured. 6. RECORDS The Borrower will record the Loan as a liability and keep a running balance in its books. 7. GOVERNING LAW This Agreement is governed by the laws of British Columbia. Signed: Lender: ____________________ Date: __________ (Yansong Zang or Yiji Wang) Borrower: ____________________ Date: __________

7. Founder Pay

All contract revenue is first paid into the company's Wealthsimple Business account (see §2.2). The company pays its own costs from that account. Money paid to a founder for his work is a dividend on his own dividend class, and nothing else.

The company must not pay either founder a salary, wage, bonus, director's fee, or management fee, and a founder does not invoice the company. Expense reimbursements, and repayment of the shareholder loans in §6, stay allowed. Those are not pay for work.

Albert Z. owns every Class A share. Jacob W. owns every Class B share. One dividend resolution sets the amount on each class. The amounts may differ, and the resolution may pay one class and none on the other. Every share inside a class is paid the same amount (s. 59(4)), which is already true because each founder owns his whole class.

Declaring the dividend is a major matter, so both classes consent before it is paid (§5). Albert Z., as director, still applies the solvency test (§4.4). If that test fails, the company does not pay either founder for that period.

The ban is written in the Articles. A founder is paid only by a dividend on his class. Changing the ban means amending the Articles, which is a major matter (§5), so neither founder can turn salary on alone. The shareholders' agreement repeats the ban, so each founder can enforce it against the other. The Articles do not adopt the Table 1 clause that lets directors set their own remuneration.

The ban binds the two founders. If the facts of the work are employment, the Canada Revenue Agency or the Employment Standards Branch can still require payroll. Dividends do not build CPP or EI, and the company cannot deduct them. Have a CPA confirm this pattern before the first dividend. Record every payment as a dividend, an expense reimbursement, or a loan repayment. An unclassified draw can be taxed as a shareholder benefit (Income Tax Act s. 15).

Informational only — not tax or legal advice. Have a lawyer write the class rights and the ban into the Articles.

11.1 What is filed, and what is kept

British Columbia does not use a separate company bylaw. The internal rules are the Articles. The online incorporation filing does not ask you to upload them. Before the filing continues, you certify that two documents already exist: a signed Incorporation Agreement and a set of Articles. Both stay in the minute book. Filing without them can leave the incorporation invalid.

What you submit through Corporate Online is the Incorporation Application and the Notice of Articles. The Notice of Articles is the short public record: name, registered office, directors, and the authorized share structure. After filing, the registry sends the Certificate of Incorporation, a certified Incorporation Application, and a certified Notice of Articles.

The ready-made rulebook is Table 1 in the Business Corporations Regulation (B.C. Reg. 65/2004, Part 15). Its purpose is to supply the ordinary operating rules — meetings, directors, officers, records, and a simple share regime — so a small company does not have to draft those clauses itself. The province's incorporation steps treat Table 1 as suitable when it is adopted unchanged. It does not create special share classes or a personal veto.

11.2 Articles for this company

This company needs one set of Articles, drafted with a BC corporate lawyer. Unmodified Table 1 is the wrong set to adopt. The sample Incorporation Agreement above only adopts Table 1 as written, so it is a pattern, not the document to sign.

The Articles have to state, in one document:

  • The two classes and their rights: Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings, receives Albert Z.'s dividend, and carries his veto; Class B does not carry the right to vote at general meetings, receives Jacob W.'s dividend, and carries his veto (see §3.2).
  • The Notice of Articles authorizes a maximum of one Class A share and one Class B share, without par value. The Incorporation Agreement issues the Class A share to Albert Z. and the Class B share to Jacob W.
  • A dividend may be declared on Class A, on Class B, or on both, in different amounts (see §7).
  • The company must not pay either founder a salary, wage, bonus, director's fee, or management fee, and must not adopt the Table 1 clause that lets directors set their own remuneration. Changing that ban is a major matter.
  • A shareholder loan is the only way a founder may put money into the company. No further share, capital contribution, gift, or other payment in is allowed. Every loan is interest-free. The lender chooses the amount and is not required to lend. On a winding-up, if both loans cannot be repaid in full, they rank equally and the remainder is split in proportion to unpaid principal (see §6). Changing this rule is a major matter.
  • Only Class A carries the right to vote at general meetings. Albert Z. holds that share (see §4).
  • Major matters. The Articles name the list in §5 and state that the company must not do any item on it unless both Class A and Class B have consented in writing. A directors' resolution does not satisfy that requirement. Class A's right to vote at general meetings does not replace the Class B consent, and Jacob W. cannot replace the Class A consent (see §4).
  • Ordinary mechanics may follow Table 1 where they do not conflict: how a directors' resolution is passed, how officers are appointed, and how records are kept.

The Incorporation Agreement is a separate signed paper. Each incorporator agrees to take the shares set out in it, and the agreement adopts this custom set of Articles. It is kept with the Articles. It is not uploaded with the filing.

Informational only — not legal advice. Have a BC corporate lawyer draft the Articles and the Incorporation Agreement before the filing certification.

The company does not act on the matter until both consents are signed. Neither consent is a general-meeting vote, and Albert Z.'s right to vote at general meetings cannot stand in for Jacob W.'s Class B consent.
