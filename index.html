Design intent

Albert Z. alone decides ordinary matters, as the sole director and the only holder of the voting shares. Profits stay 50/50 through the dividend shares. A major matter needs the consent of both veto classes, so neither founder can do it alone and neither can outvote the other. Both veto shares are created at incorporation (§4 and §5).

4. Share Structure

The company uses four share classes. Ordinary voting, dividends, and the two personal vetoes are kept apart so that a right given to one founder is not a right the other can exercise. This is a draft to be finalized in the Articles with a lawyer (see §14).

Class Rights Albert Z. Jacob W. Purpose
Class A — Voting Votes on ordinary shareholder matters. No dividend. 100% None Albert Z. alone passes ordinary shareholder resolutions
Class B — Dividend No ordinary vote. Entitled to dividends and return of capital. 50% 50% Economics (50/50)
Class C — Albert Z.'s veto No ordinary vote and no dividend. The company must not do a major matter, or change this veto, without this class's consent. Sole owner (one share) None Albert Z.'s personal block on major matters
Class D — Jacob W.'s veto Same veto right as Class C, in a separate class. No ordinary vote and no dividend. None Sole owner (one share) Jacob W.'s personal block on major matters
BCA — Supported

The BCA does not use fixed "common/preferred" categories. Each share carries the special rights or restrictions set in the Articles, which may relate to voting, dividends, and return of capital (ss. 59–60). Separate classes for ordinary votes, dividends, and vetoes are therefore permitted.

Within a single class, every share must carry the same rights (s. 59(4)). Albert Z.'s veto and Jacob W.'s veto are different classes so that one founder cannot vote the other's veto. Changing a class's rights needs a special separate resolution of that class (s. 61). Both veto shares are issued at incorporation. Creating, cancelling, or changing a veto class later is a major matter and needs the consent of both existing veto classes (§6).

5. Decision-Making & Voting

Three different records cover three different kinds of decision. The same person may sign more than one of them. The minute book should name which one it is.

  • Ordinary mattersAlbert Z. alone, as sole director. If the Act requires a shareholder resolution, he also passes it alone because he owns 100% of the Class A voting shares. Jacob W.'s agreement is not required.
  • Major mattersWritten consent of both veto classes, Class C and Class D (see §6). Albert Z.'s Class A votes cannot replace Jacob W.'s consent, and Jacob W. cannot replace Albert Z.'s.
  • DividendsDeclaring a dividend is a major matter. Once declared, Class B is paid 50/50 (see §8).

5.1 Board meetings

A board meeting is a meeting of the directors. The directors manage the company, or supervise that management (BCA s. 136). They appoint officers and set their duties (s. 141). Ordinary business is decided here: client work, delivery, spending inside the ordinary course, and the other items that are not on the major-matter list.

This company has one director, Albert Z. Under s. 140(4), the sole director may constitute a board meeting by himself. He may also sign a written directors' resolution instead of holding a meeting (s. 140(3)). There is no second director and no tie to break.

Jacob W. does not attend as a director and has no vote at a board meeting. His President and CTO work is officer work done under the board's authority (§7). It is not a board vote.

5.2 Shareholder meetings

A shareholder meeting is a meeting of the owners of shares that are allowed to vote on that matter. Shareholders do not manage the day-to-day business. They decide items the Act or the Articles reserve to them, and they elect the director. The company holds an annual general meeting unless the voting shareholders waive it (s. 182, and §8).

On an ordinary shareholder matter, only Class A votes. Albert Z. owns every Class A share, so he alone can pass the resolution. Jacob W.'s dividend shares and his veto share do not vote. His absence cannot stop the meeting, and his disagreement is not a vote on that resolution.

Albert Z. is often the only person involved in both a board decision and a shareholder decision. They are still different acts. A directors' resolution is him acting as director. A Class A shareholder resolution is him acting as the holder of the voting shares. Each one is written down separately.

5.3 Veto consents

A major matter is not a board vote and not a Class A vote. The Articles provide that the company must not do it unless both veto classes consent in writing. Class C is Albert Z.'s consent. Class D is Jacob W.'s consent. Each class has one holder, so one signature is that class's consent, and the other person's signature cannot replace it.

This is why the vetoes are separate classes. Shares inside one class must have the same rights (s. 59(4)). If both founders held veto shares of a single class, a class meeting could count only the votes cast there, and one founder might be able to pass it without the other. Separate classes mean each consent stands on its own.

Attention — have the lawyer entrench both vetoes

The Articles need to say that the sole director cannot do a listed major matter without both veto consents. Section 137 allows the Articles to move particular director powers. Section 61 protects a class when its own rights are changed. The drafting has to close the path on which one founder amends the Articles, or holds a meeting the other skips, and thereby drops the other founder's veto. Both veto classes are issued on the day of incorporation so that neither consent is something the other founder can withhold later.

A shareholders' agreement can repeat the list and set the remedy if someone ignores it. British Columbia has no federal-style unanimous shareholders' agreement that removes directors' duties. Albert Z., as director, still has to act honestly and in the company's interests, with reasonable care (s. 142).

Informational only — not legal advice. A BC corporate lawyer should draft the Articles before incorporation.

Unless the Articles say otherwise, any individual may hold an office, and one person may hold more than one office. BCA — Supported

A BC company needs at least one director (s. 120), and BC imposes no residency or citizenship requirement on directors (s. 124). Albert Z. can be the sole director. A director must be an individual aged 18 or over, capable of managing their own affairs, and not disqualified (for example, certain fraud convictions or an undischarged bankruptcy). Directors, and officers when acting as officers, owe duties of honesty, loyalty, and care to the company (s. 142). Jacob W. can be President and CTO without being a director (s. 141).

8. Meetings, Profit & Dividends

Each year the company holds an Annual General Meeting unless every Class A voting shareholder waives it (BCA s. 182). Albert Z. owns all of those shares, so he can hold the meeting alone or sign the waiver. The meeting is for the business the Act puts to voting shareholders, such as the annual financial statements and election of the director. It is not a board meeting, and Jacob W. does not vote at it.

8.1 Annual allocation of net profit

  • Company development reserve — setting the reserve is a major matter, so both veto classes must consent (§6)
  • Dividends — declaring a dividend is a major matter. Once declared, Class B is paid 50/50
Attention — solvency test on dividends

Under BCA s. 70, the company must not declare or pay a dividend if there are reasonable grounds to believe it is insolvent (unable to pay debts as they come due) or that the dividend would render it insolvent; it also needs sufficient retained earnings. Directors who approve an offside dividend can be personally liable. Dividends must follow class rights — equal within Class B, i.e., 50/50. Declaring the dividend is a major matter, so both veto classes consent before it is paid (§6).

The "development reserve" is an internal retained-earnings allocation (a bookkeeping decision), not a separate legal entity.

MethodHow it is recordedNotes
Share subscription (equity) Increases paid-up capital (PUC) Return of capital up to PUC is generally tax-free but reduces PUC; equity is harder to withdraw (needs return of capital, redemption, or dividend).

Document everything: subscription agreements, a directors' resolution issuing the shares, and shareholder-loan agreements, all recorded in the minute book and the books of account. A capital injection is not revenue, so no GST/PST applies. Keep the paid-up capital deliberate (it affects future tax-free capital returns), and track each founder's contributions and loans in a register. Confirm the equity-vs-loan mix and PUC with a CPA.

Also confirm current Wealthsimple business-account eligibility/terms; keep an alternative business bank in mind as a fallback.

10. Revenue Sharing & Founder Compensation

All contract revenue is first paid into the company's Wealthsimple Business account. For each project, the founders agree a reasonable split reflecting each person's contribution across lead generation/negotiation, development, delivery, and after-sales support, and each draws an agreed amount to their personal account. The remaining funds stay in the company: part covers other project costs, and the rest becomes company profit for the AGM (§8).

Attention — this is the key compliance/tax area

Contribution-based, variable payments to the founders cannot be treated as dividends: dividends within a share class must be equal per share (s. 59(4)), and the Class B dividend is fixed at 50/50 — so effort-based, unequal amounts must be characterized as compensation for services, not dividends.

Proper options: (a) Salary/wages — needs a CRA payroll account and source deductions (income tax, CPP; EI generally does not apply to owners with > 40% voting control) with T4 slips; or (b) Contractor / management fees — each founder invoices the company and reports self-employment income (consider GST registration). Both are deductible business expenses to the company.

Avoid undocumented "withdrawals" to personal accounts: every draw must be classified (salary, contractor fee, expense reimbursement, dividend, or shareholder-loan repayment) and recorded, or it risks being taxed as a shareholder benefit / deemed dividend (ITA s. 15). Recommendation: adopt a written compensation policy, pay effort-based splits as salary or contractor fees, keep dividends separate and equal (50/50), reconcile monthly, and have a CPA set up payroll/contractor processes.

14.1 What is filed, and what is kept

British Columbia does not use a separate company bylaw. The internal rules are the Articles. The online incorporation filing does not ask you to upload them. Before the filing continues, you certify that two documents already exist: a signed Incorporation Agreement and a set of Articles. Both stay in the minute book. Filing without them can leave the incorporation invalid.

What you submit through Corporate Online is the Incorporation Application and the Notice of Articles. The Notice of Articles is the short public record: name, registered office, directors, and the authorized share structure. After filing, the registry sends the Certificate of Incorporation, a certified Incorporation Application, and a certified Notice of Articles.

The ready-made rulebook is Table 1 in the Business Corporations Regulation (B.C. Reg. 65/2004, Part 15). Its purpose is to supply the ordinary operating rules — meetings, directors, officers, records, and a simple share regime — so a small company does not have to draft those clauses itself. The province's incorporation steps treat Table 1 as suitable when it is adopted unchanged. It does not create special share classes or a personal veto.

14.2 Articles for this company

This company needs one set of Articles, drafted with a BC corporate lawyer. Unmodified Table 1 is the wrong set to adopt. The sample Incorporation Agreement above only adopts Table 1 as written, so it is a pattern, not the document to sign.

The Articles have to state, in one document:

  • The four classes and their rights: Class A votes and pays no dividend; Class B is the 50/50 dividend and does not vote; Class C is Albert Z.'s veto share; Class D is Jacob W.'s veto share (see §4).
  • Only Class A votes at a general meeting. Albert Z. owns every Class A share (see §5).
  • A listed major matter needs the written consent of both Class C and Class D. The sole director cannot do that matter alone (see §6 and §5).
  • Ordinary mechanics may follow Table 1 where they do not conflict: how a directors' resolution is passed, how officers are appointed, and how records are kept.

The Incorporation Agreement is a separate signed paper. Each incorporator agrees to take the shares set out in it, and the agreement adopts this custom set of Articles. It is kept with the Articles. It is not uploaded with the filing.

Informational only — not legal advice. Have a BC corporate lawyer draft the Articles and the Incorporation Agreement before the filing certification.
